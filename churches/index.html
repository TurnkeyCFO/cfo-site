The churches who trust us with their books
We know what Tuesday looks like

The books are three weeks behind. Again.

There is an invoice sitting in the pastor's text messages waiting on a yes. Someone on staff paid for something out of pocket in April and is still waiting. A board member asked a straight question last month and nobody in the room could answer it.

None of that means your people are bad at their jobs. It means nobody was ever given a finance function to run. You have been holding it together with a part time bookkeeper, a spreadsheet, and goodwill.

  • "I don't really know what our giving looks like."
  • "I don't have a finance background and I won't pretend I do."
  • "I know we need to fix this. I don't know where to start."
  • "I don't want to handhold another bookkeeper."
  • "I'm going to end up figuring this out myself."

You don't have a bookkeeping problem. You have a missing department problem.

A bookkeeper records what already happened. That is one job. A finance function decides who can approve a payment, whether a restricted gift stayed restricted, how a reimbursement gets back to a volunteer, whether your housing allowance was documented before it was paid, and what your board sees before it votes.

Hiring one part time person and expecting all of that was never going to work. Every church has tried it. The problem is structural, not personal, and it is why the same nine things are broken at almost every church we look at.

What a finance function actually covers

Ten pillars. A bookkeeper covers one.

This is what we grade in your audit, and it is the fastest way to see the size of the gap between what you have today and what your church actually needs.

1

Bill pay and approvals

Who is allowed to approve what, and can you prove it later.Broken: invoices approved by text. Pastor is the bottleneck.

2

Reimbursements and expense cards

Staff and volunteers made whole quickly, with receipts attached.Broken: people paying out of pocket and waiting. Card charges with no memo.

3

Payroll and clergy compensation

Ministers classified correctly and housing allowance documented properly.Broken: housing allowance never set by board resolution.

4

Bookkeeping and monthly close

Books closed on a fixed calendar, not whenever there is time.This is the one a bookkeeper covers. It is one of ten.

5

Software and systems

Giving, accounting and payroll actually talking to each other.Broken: manual re-entry every week between three systems.

6

Budget

A budget the board can track against through the year.Broken: approved in January, never opened again in June.

7

Forecast and cash runway

Knowing how many months of operating cash you are holding.Broken: nobody has ever calculated it.

8

Annual giving statements

Accurate, IRS-compliant statements out to every giver in January, reconciled to the books.Broken: statements that do not tie to deposits, sent late or by hand.

9

Financial statements and board reporting

Reporting your board can read, with fund detail and context.Broken: a raw export handed over with no explanation.

10

1099s and compliance

W-9s collected up front and filings made on time.Broken: contractors paid with no W-9 on file. Filings late or missed.

Honest answers

The questions pastors actually ask us.

We already have a bookkeeper.
Good, and we are not trying to take anything from them. The audit will show you what your bookkeeper does not cover, and most of the ten pillars sit outside that job by design. Plenty of churches keep their bookkeeper and have us install everything around them.
Is this really free?
Yes, and there is no obligation attached to it. Here is what we get out of it: some churches read the assessment and hire us, most do not, and we would rather be the firm that gave you a straight answer. You keep the assessment and the dashboard either way.
How can you assess us that quickly?
Because we have built the finance function for churches enough times to know exactly what to look for, and we have a system for assembling it. The speed is the point. If a firm takes two weeks to send you a proposal, that is a preview of how quickly they will move once you are a client.
We are too small for this.
The smaller the staff, the more of the finance work the pastor is personally doing, and the more this matters. Small churches are not a lighter version of this problem. They are usually the sharpest version of it, because one person is doing six jobs.
Do you replace our staff?
No. We install the function around the people you already have. In most cases your staff end up doing less finance work and more of what you actually hired them for.
Do you do our taxes?
No. We work alongside your CPA and hand them clean books. Tax and legal questions go to your CPA or attorney, and we are happy to talk to them directly so you are not the messenger.
How can you guarantee clarity? That sounds subjective.
It would be, which is why we defined it as four things you can check yourself on day 30: your books are current and reconciled, your giving and funds and budget versus actual live on one dashboard, your systems are consolidated with a written blueprint, and you can answer any board question from that dashboard in under five minutes. If any one of those is not true, you get a full refund.
Our books are badly behind. Is that a problem?
It is the normal starting point. We have begun with churches two years behind, and nothing you tell us is going to shock us. Catch-up work is quoted separately based on how far behind the books actually are, and the audit tells you exactly where that stands before you commit to anything.
